---
name: dont-make-me-think
type: principle
description: Every screen should be self-evident — a user shouldn't have to stop and figure out how to use it, only what it does for them.
---

A well-designed screen answers its own questions before the user has to
ask them: what is this, what can I do here, why would I want to. If a
user has to stop, study the layout, and reason out how something works,
that's a design failure — even if they eventually succeed. Krug (*Don't
Make Me Think*) calls this the First Law of Usability: a page should be
self-evident, or as close to it as you can get.

This is a stricter bar than "the user can figure it out." Figuring it
out costs attention that was supposed to go toward the user's actual
task. Every "wait, what does this button do" is attention borrowed from
somewhere else and rarely paid back.

In practice: label controls by what they do, not a clever or generic
name; put the next action where the eye already is, not where it's
merely reachable; make the obvious interpretation of a layout the
correct one, so a user's first guess works.

**Why it matters:** users don't evaluate a design in the abstract — they
hit friction at the exact moment they're trying to get something done,
and a design that makes them think is a design that's failing them at
the worst possible moment to fail.
