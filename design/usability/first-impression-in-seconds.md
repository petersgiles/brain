---
name: first-impression-in-seconds
type: principle
description: The first few seconds on any screen must answer three questions on their own — what is this, what can I do here, why should I care — or the user leaves before finding out.
---

A user landing on a screen for the first time is silently asking three
questions, and a design has seconds, not minutes, to answer all three
before they bounce: What is this? What can I do here? Why should I be
here rather than somewhere else? Krug (*Don't Make Me Think*) calls
getting this right the "Big Bang" — the first impression is doing more
work than any other single moment in the experience.

This is a stronger requirement than "the information is present
somewhere on the page." A tagline in small type below the fold answers
the question technically but not in time — by the time it's read, the
user has already formed an impression from the parts that were visible
first: the name, the dominant image, the first line of text, the overall
visual tone.

In practice: put identity and purpose in the first thing the eye lands
on, not the third; let the visual style itself signal what kind of thing
this is (a tool, a store, a document) before any copy is read; make the
one primary action obvious rather than presenting several competing
calls to action with equal visual weight.

**Why it matters:** a user who can't answer these three questions
quickly doesn't stay to look harder — they leave and try somewhere else,
so a slow or buried answer costs the user entirely rather than costing
them a few extra seconds.
